    Where we start

    Every job board says "AI PM."
    Almost none of them mean the same thing.

    Posting A

    "Own our LLM assistant"

    Model behaviour, retrieval quality, evals, hallucination rate. The model is the product.

    Posting B

    "AI-first PM, ship 3× faster"

    Classic product. They want someone who uses AI as a power tool, not someone who builds AI.

    Posting C

    "AI platform PM"

    Internal infra: gateways, prompt registries, eval tooling, GPU cost. Your users are engineers.

    Three completely different jobs. Same two letters. If you don't disambiguate this first, everything after it is noise.

    Clearing the ground

    What an AI PM is not

    Myth 01

    "A PM who uses ChatGPT"

    Writing your PRD faster is a productivity habit, not a role. Most PMs already do this by default.

    RealityTable stakes, not a title.
    Myth 02

    A data scientist or ML engineer

    You don't train models and you don't tune hyperparameters. You read the numbers and decide what they mean for the user.

    RealityYou don't produce the metric. You decide what it has to be.
    Myth 03

    A prompt engineer

    A prompt is an implementation detail that changes with every model release. Owning the prompt is not owning the product.

    RealityPrompts are a lever. Evals are the contract.
    Myth 04

    A brand-new profession

    It's product management — with a failure surface that classic PM training never covered.

    RealityThe old skills are still required. They're just no longer sufficient.

    Notice the pattern: every myth is a tool, a tactic or a title. None of them is an outcome. That's the tell — the job is the outcome.

    The definition worth memorising

    An AI PM owns the outcome of a system that is probabilistic, data-dependent, and never finished.

    Probabilistic

    The same input can return a different output. "It works" stops being a fact and becomes a statistic.

    SoYou ship a pass rate, never a guarantee.

    Data-dependent

    Quality comes from data and context, not from more code. No data, no product.

    SoYour first sprint is a data plan, not a prompt.

    Never finished

    Shipping is the start. Models drift, users adapt, providers deprecate. The loop never closes.

    SoBudget for the loop, not for a launch.

    Read it again with the emphasis on owns. Not "contributes to," not "coordinates." When the output is wrong on an ordinary Tuesday, it is your name on it.

    The single idea this whole talk hangs on

    You stopped shipping rules.
    You started shipping behaviour.

    Classic software
    if (user.plan === 'pro')
      → unlock()

    Same input → same output.
    Always. Forever.
    AI software
    model(prompt + context)
      → a distribution of answers

    Same input → e.g. 94% great,
    5% mediocre, 1% embarrassing
    — and you never know
    which one this user got.

    Your job is that last slice. Not the happy path — the tail. Classic PM skills get you to a convincing demo; the tail decides whether the demo ever becomes a product. And the tail is invisible unless somebody goes looking for it. That somebody is you.

    Pick your lane before you apply

    Three jobs hiding under one title

    Type 1 · the model is the product

    AI Product PM

    You own: answer quality, retrieval, evals, hallucination rate, guardrails, cost per task.

    Looks like: a support copilot, a coding agent, a document assistant.

    Hardest part: deciding what "good" means.

    Tell in the ad"evals", "retrieval", "hallucination", "model quality"
    Type 2 · AI as leverage

    AI-Native PM

    You own: a normal product — but you research, prototype, analyse and write with AI in the loop.

    Looks like: a PM shipping clickable prototypes on day one instead of week three.

    Hardest part: not confusing speed with judgement.

    Tell in the ad"AI-first", "ship faster" — but no model inside the product
    Type 3 · picks and shovels

    AI Platform PM

    You own: model gateway, eval tooling, prompt versioning, observability, spend controls.

    Looks like: internal infra. Your users have engineering titles.

    Hardest part: proving value nobody sees.

    Tell in the ad"platform", "developer experience", "internal tooling", "spend"

    Ask which one you're interviewing for in the first call — the answer changes the whole conversation. This talk is mostly about Type 1, because that's where the genuinely new skills live.

    Your technical floor

    Five layers you must be able to argue about

    05Feedback loopTraces, thumbs, evals, error taxonomy, dashboards. The layer 90% of teams skip — and the only one that compounds.
    04Product surfaceHow uncertainty is shown, corrected and undone. Where a mediocre model becomes a good product — or the reverse.
    03OrchestrationSingle call vs. chain vs. agent. Tool use, routing, retries, human handoff. Mostly a product decision, not an engineering one.
    02ContextRAG, search, memory, system prompt, structured tool outputs. Where the majority of real quality wins actually come from.
    01ModelWhich one, at what cost, at what latency, with what context window. A product decision with a price tag.

    You don't have to build any of it — you do have to hold an opinion on every layer. Practical test: when "the answer was wrong" lands in your inbox, name the layer before anyone touches the model. Most of the time it's layer 02 — the right document was never retrieved, so no model could have answered.

    Job 1 of 6

    Choosing where AI is actually worth it

    Green light
    • High volume, repetitive work
    • Messy, unstructured input — text, audio, images, PDFs
    • An expensive human step in the middle
    • An error is annoying, not catastrophic
    • A human can verify the output in seconds
    Red light
    • The answer must be right 100% of the time
    • A regex or a lookup table already solves it
    • You have no data and no path to any
    • The volume is 40 requests a month
    • Nobody can tell whether the output is good

    Two questions that settle it in any kickoff. First: if a smart intern did this by hand, would we pay them? If no, AI won't rescue it — it will just make the wrong thing faster. Second: when it's wrong, who pays — the user, the company, or a regulator? That answer sets your quality bar before a single prompt gets written.

    Job 2 of 6

    Data strategy — and the cold-start problem

    Everyone wants the data flywheel. Almost nobody plans the first turn. Rough calibration: ~200 labelled examples buy you an eval set you can argue with; ~1,000 buy you one you can trust.

    Source 01

    Dogfood

    Your own team produces the first 200 real examples. Unglamorous. Fastest.

    CostA week of the team's time
    Source 02

    Design partners

    3–5 customers who trade their data for early access and roadmap influence.

    CostRoadmap debt you'll repay
    Source 03

    Synthetic

    Generate the edge cases you can't wait for. Great for evals, risky as training data.

    CostConfidence in a world you invented
    Source 04

    Human-in-the-loop

    Ship with a reviewer. Every correction is a labelled example. This is the flywheel.

    CostSlower launch, compounding returns

    And the question you should be able to answer before legal asks it: whose data is this, what were those users told, and are we allowed to keep it? A "no" discovered in month six deletes your training set.

    Job 3 of 6 · the one that matters most

    Evals are the new PRD.

    You stop describing the behaviour you want and start building the test suite that proves you got it — written before the first prompt. In a probabilistic system, a description isn't a spec. Evidence is.

    Rule 01

    Binary beats scores

    "Pass / fail" is reproducible. "Rate this 1–5" is a debate. Split fuzzy quality into many small yes/no questions.

    Rule 02

    Look at the data

    Read 100 real outputs by hand. No substitute, no shortcut. This is the highest-leverage hour in the job.

    Rule 03

    Validate your judge

    Using an LLM to grade? Check it against human labels first. An unvalidated judge is a confident random number generator.

    "Was the answer good?" → unmeasurable, unarguable, useless
    ──────── break it into checks a stranger could run ────────
    cites_a_real_source ··· pass   answers_the_actual_question ··· pass
    invents_no_facts ······ fail   refuses_when_unsupported ···· pass
    How quality actually improves

    The loop you'll run every single week

    01 Ship
    Small, behind a flag, to real users
    02 Capture
    Log every trace: input, context, output
    03 Read
    Open 100 of them. Manually.
    04 Cluster
    Group failures into a named taxonomy
    05 Fix
    Context? Prompt? Model? UX?
    06 Lock
    Add an eval so it can't come back

    What most teams do instead

    Try a new prompt → run the demo → "feels better" → ship → repeat. That's a vibe check. It doesn't compound, and six months in you have nothing that shows you moved forward.

    Why the loop wins

    Every fix becomes permanent, so quality only goes up. Six months in, your eval suite is the moat — not the model, which your competitor rents from the same provider.

    Cadence: one full turn per week. If a turn takes a month, you don't have a loop — you have a project.

    Job 4 of 6

    Designing for a product that is sometimes wrong

    Set expectations

    Say it's AI. Say it can be wrong. Trust survives errors; it does not survive surprises.

    Make it correctable

    Edit in place, not "try again." The user's fix is your best training signal.

    Show your work

    Citations, sources, the steps taken. Verifiable beats confident.

    Cheap undo

    Never let a wrong output do something irreversible without a human.

    Fail gracefully

    "I'm not sure" is a feature. Design the low-confidence path first, not last.

    Same model, two products. Autocomplete shows greyed-out text you accept with Tab — a wrong suggestion costs nothing, so a ~26% acceptance rate still felt like a productivity win in GitHub's Copilot study. Put the identical model behind a "Generate" button that overwrites your file and three misses in four is broken. The model didn't change; the cost of being wrong did. So the design question isn't "is the model good enough" — it's "what does a wrong answer cost this user, right here?"

    Job 5 of 6

    The trade-off you now own personally

    Quality
    Is the answer good?
    Cost
    Cents per request × millions
    Latency
    Seconds the user waits

    Why this is yours, not engineering's

    "Bigger model, 3× the price, 4% better on our eval" is not a technical question. It's pricing, margin and positioning — and only the PM sits where those three meet.

    The question behind itWhich 4% of users notice, and do they pay us?

    The move nobody teaches you

    Route by difficulty: the easy 80% goes to a small cheap model, the expensive one handles only the hard tail. Typical outcome — spend roughly halves, eval pass rate barely moves.

    When teams find itThe month after the first real invoice
    Job 6 of 6

    Trust, safety and the new P0

    Confidently wrong

    Hallucination isn't a bug you close. It's a rate you manage.

    First controlGround it, cite it, and score "I don't know" above a confident guess

    Prompt injection

    Any untrusted text your model reads is an instruction from someone. Web pages, emails, PDFs, tickets.

    First controlRetrieved text never carries authority

    Data leakage

    What enters the context window, who can see it, where the vendor stores it.

    First controlWrite the retention answer before legal asks

    Bias & fairness

    The model inherits the world's patterns, including the ones you'd never ship on purpose.

    First controlSlice every eval by the segments you serve

    Regulation

    EU AI Act, sector rules, disclosure duties. Your risk tier sets the roadmap, not the reverse. Annex III high-risk duties now apply from Dec 2027 — deferred, not dropped.

    First controlClassify your tier in week one, not month nine

    Brand risk

    One screenshot of your product saying something awful travels further than any launch you'll ever run.

    First controlA refusal is cheaper than a screenshot
    Measurement

    One scorecard is no longer enough

    System metrics · does the model work?
    • Eval pass rate — per failure category
    • Hallucination / unsupported-claim rate
    • p50 and p95 latency — averages lie
    • Cost per completed task
    • Refusal & fallback rate
    Product metrics · does it matter?
    • Task completion rate
    • Time saved per task — vs. the old way
    • Repeat usage & retention
    • Willingness to pay
    • Support load created — or removed

    The metric that connects both sides: acceptance rate

    What share of outputs does the user keep without editing? It says "the model is useful" and "the user agrees" at the same time. If you can track exactly one thing, track this — next to a guardrail like critical-error rate, because acceptance measures usefulness, not correctness. And watch the trap: if eval pass rate climbs every week while acceptance rate stays flat, you are improving the test, not the product.

    Reality check

    What the week actually looks like

    Mon
    Read 50 production traces from the weekend. Tag every failure. Update the taxonomy.
    Tue
    Eval review with engineering. Two categories regressed after the retrieval change. Decide what ships.
    Wed
    Experiment readout. New chunking strategy: +6% on citations, −0.4s latency. Ship it.
    Thu
    Cost review. 71% of spend is on requests a smaller model handles fine. Write the routing spec.
    Fri
    Design review — but only on the failure states. Low confidence, empty results, refusals.

    Notice what's missing: the quarterly roadmap deck. It still exists — it's just no longer the centre of gravity. In this job, quality is the roadmap.

    Skills

    Learn this. Skip that.

    Learn — high return
    • Evals & error analysis — the #1 skill, no contest
    • RAG fundamentals — chunking, embeddings, why retrieval fails
    • Tokens, context windows, cost and latency maths
    • Tool calling & agent loops — and when not to use one
    • AI UX patterns — steal shamelessly from good products
    • Reading an eval report without needing a translator
    Proof you've learned itYou can hand someone an eval report and the decision it drove
    Skip — low return for a PM
    • Training or fine-tuning your own base model
    • Transformer maths and attention internals
    • GPU procurement and infrastructure
    • Chasing every new model release on launch day
    • Public leaderboard rank as a decision — build an eval on your own task instead
    • Prompt "tricks" that die with the next version
    Why skip themNone of them changes a decision you'll actually have to make
    Starting Monday

    A 90-day plan that actually works

    Days 1–30 · See
    • Build one small AI feature end-to-end yourself
    • Read 100 real outputs of an AI product you use daily
    • Write down every failure you see, in your own words
    • Learn the vocabulary: token, context, RAG, eval, trace
    DeliverableA written failure list nobody had before you
    Days 31–60 · Measure
    • Turn that failure list into a named taxonomy
    • Write 20 binary evals — a spreadsheet is genuinely fine
    • Run them against two different models
    • Compute cost and p95 latency for one real task
    DeliverableA two-model comparison with numbers, not opinions
    Days 61–90 · Ship
    • Pick one workflow and improve it against your evals
    • Design the failure states before the happy path
    • Publish a short write-up: problem → loop → numbers
    • Show what got worse, not only what got better
    DeliverableA public write-up with a before and an after

    That write-up is your portfolio. In a field where everyone claims AI experience, the person who can show a measured before-and-after is holding the only credential that survives an interview.

    Learn from other people's scars

    Six ways AI products die

    Demo-driven development

    It worked in the demo because you picked the input. Production picks its own.

    Early warningThe demo inputs live in a doc nobody updates

    Vibe checks as QA

    "Feels better" is not a measurement. You can't improve what three people disagree about.

    Early warningNobody can name last week's pass rate

    Model obsession

    Six weeks comparing models when the real problem was the workflow around them.

    Early warningThe roadmap is a list of model names

    Cost discovered late

    Unit economics that work right up until someone actually uses the feature.

    Early warningNo one owns a cost-per-task number

    No human fallback

    Confidence 100% of the time, in a system that's right 85% of the time.

    Early warningThere's no "I'm not sure" state in the designs

    AI looking for a problem

    The feature exists because a competitor shipped one. Users never asked, and it shows.

    Early warningThe rationale opens with a competitor's name
    If you remember one sentence

    AI didn't make product management easier.

    It moved the hard part from "what should we build" to "is this actually any good."

    Anyone can ship an AI feature in a weekend now. Almost nobody can tell you whether theirs is any good — and prove it with numbers. That gap is the entire job.
